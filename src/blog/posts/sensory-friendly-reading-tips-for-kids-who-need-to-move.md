You open a picture book together. Before the second page, your child is upside down on the couch, rocking, humming, or walking a lap around the room.

That does not automatically mean the story has lost them.

Some children listen best when their bodies are moving. A quiet body is not the only sign of attention, and reading together does not have to look like sitting still with hands folded. The goal is connection with the story—not performing one “correct” reading posture.

## Let movement be part of the story

If your child likes to rock, pace, bounce gently, or change positions, try reading without repeatedly asking them to stop. Pause occasionally and ask an easy question about the story. Their answer may show that they heard far more than their movement suggested.

Movement can also become play. Stomp when a character stomps. Stretch when someone reaches for the sky. Act out a slow walk, a fast run, or a deep breath. This turns reading into a shared experience instead of a test of stillness.

## Give busy hands something simple to do

A familiar fidget, soft toy, drawing pad, or small piece of modeling dough can help some children stay with a read-aloud. Keep the choice simple so the object supports the story instead of competing with it.

If your child uses specialized sensory tools, follow the guidance of the professional who knows their individual needs. There is no universal tool or weight that is right for every child.

## Make coming and going welcome

Some children listen for a minute, move away, and return for the next page. You can keep reading in a calm voice and leave space for them to rejoin. A short, positive read-aloud is more valuable than a longer session that becomes a struggle.

Try stopping while the experience still feels good. Finishing every page is not required. Returning tomorrow is part of building a reading life too.

## Experiment with the setting

The couch is only one option. Try a blanket fort, a quiet corner, the floor, a porch swing, or a spot under the table. Reduce background noise when you can, and let your child choose between two comfortable places.

It can also help to preview the plan: “We’ll read one story, take a movement break, and then decide whether we want another.” Knowing what comes next can make the experience feel more predictable.

## Choose books that leave room to breathe

Some readers enjoy busy, detailed pages. Others prefer clear illustrations, predictable pacing, and less visual clutter. Notice which books your child returns to and what those books have in common.
